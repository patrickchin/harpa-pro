An interior project can involve furniture from one supplier, lighting
from another, and hardware from a third. Consolidation brings those
orders to an agreed facility before onward transport. It can simplify
receiving and make a combined freight booking possible.

The benefit depends on the whole cost. Inland collection, storage,
repacking, documentation, and delays all belong in the comparison.
Ask for a written plan before giving suppliers a warehouse address.

## Decide what you are combining

Prepare a shipment register with a separate line for each supplier and
purchase order. Include the product, quantity, carton count, packed
dimensions, gross weight, and readiness date. Mark which figures are
estimates and who will confirm them.

A buyer's consolidation and an LCL ocean booking solve related problems.
The first brings together your supplier orders; the second can place
your cargo alongside other customers' goods in a shared container. Your
combined order might ultimately travel by LCL, a dedicated container,
air freight, or separate services.

Maersk's LCL guidance describes collection, consolidation, international
movement, and destination separation. Ask the chosen operator which
services they will perform for your order.
[Maersk's LCL process](https://www.maersk.com/support/faqs/2023/08/12/how-does-maersk-lcl-work)

## Choose a facility around the supply plan

Map the supplier locations before choosing the receiving warehouse.
A cheap warehouse can become expensive if every supplier needs a long
inland movement. Request collection costs from the actual addresses.

Confirm the operator's legal entity, warehouse address, contact details,
receiving hours, and the parties carrying out transport and storage.
Check that the facility can handle the products and packed weights.
Ask about access controls, storage conditions, and how cargo is identified.

Resolve these questions in writing:

- Who books each supplier delivery and pays its inland freight?
- Which receiving and storage charges apply, and when do they start?
- Can the warehouse open cartons, photograph goods, or host an inspector?
- Who authorises repacking, repairs, disposal, or release?
- Which liability terms and insurance arrangements cover warehouse storage?
- Who prepares export documents and books international transport?

A receiving service does not automatically include a product inspection.
Ask for each task separately and agree the evidence you will receive.

## Give every supplier the same delivery instructions

Issue a warehouse instruction sheet with the buyer's reference, supplier
name, purchase-order number, carton sequence, receiving contact, and
booking procedure. Require a packing list before dispatch.

Use marks that remain readable after handling. For example, a lighting
supplier's cartons could identify the purchase order and show carton 1
of 8 through carton 8 of 8. The warehouse needs to distinguish that batch
from another buyer's identical-looking cartons.

Ask each supplier to send the dispatch date, carrier reference, carton
count, and loading photographs. Share those with the warehouse before
arrival. A message saying “sent to your agent” leaves too much unresolved.

## Define what happens at receiving

Build a receiving report for each delivery. It should record the arrival
date, supplier, transport reference, number of packages, visible condition,
and discrepancies against the packing list. Require photographs showing
the carton marks and any damage.

Opening every carton may be appropriate for some orders. Others need an
agreed sampling or specialist inspection plan. Checking eight boxes
arrived cannot establish whether eighty light fittings have the specified
electrical components.

Keep uncertain goods on hold. Identify who investigates shortages and
whether the supplier should replace goods before the consolidated shipment
is released. Agree who pays any additional storage and handling.

## Set a latest receiving date

The slowest supplier can delay the complete order. Maintain a live
readiness schedule with four distinct dates: production complete,
inspection accepted, goods delivered to the warehouse, and shipment
released for booking.

Set a latest receiving date based on the transport booking and project
needs. Agree in advance how to handle a late supplier. The choices may
include delaying the shipment, sending a partial order, or routing the
late goods separately.

For a hypothetical project, furniture could be ready before the matching
handles. The buyer should compare storage and project-delay costs with
the cost of shipping the handles separately. The cheapest freight line
alone will not answer that question.

## Plan repacking and loading

Request a proposed packing and loading plan once the actual quantities
are known. Discuss stackability, fragile surfaces, heavy items, moisture
sensitivity, and unloading sequence. Ask for revised dimensions and
weights after repacking.

Check the requirements for wooden packaging on the route. ISPM 15
addresses the phytosanitary treatment and marking of regulated wood
packaging in international trade; check the material and destination
before accepting crates or pallets.
[IPPC's ISPM 15 guidance](https://www.ippc.int/en/archive-old-pages/phytosanitary-system/ispm-15-implementation/)

Have the operator record the final package count, marks, seal details
where applicable, and loading photographs. These records help the
receiver identify goods and investigate damage or shortages later.

## Keep the commercial records traceable

Combining goods physically does not remove the need for accurate
product descriptions, values, origin information, and applicable
classification. Give the forwarder and destination customs representative
the supplier invoices and product details early.

Ask them to confirm the invoicing, exporter, consignee, and declaration
arrangements for your shipment. The suitable structure depends on the
transaction and jurisdictions. Keep a clear link between each supplier
invoice, purchase order, packing-list line, and final shipping mark.

Request the final document set for review before dispatch. Resolve
inconsistent quantities or descriptions while the goods remain accessible.

## Approve a combined cost and release record

Compare separate shipments with consolidation using the same delivery
scope. Include collection, receiving, inspections, storage, repacking,
export handling, international freight, destination handling, clearance,
and final delivery.

Before release, sign off the supplier register against the final packing
list. Record any goods deliberately left behind. Give the receiving team
a copy and identify who will answer questions during clearance and
delivery. Your consolidation is ready when both the goods and their
records match the order you intend to ship.

checks to complete before releasing the consolidated shipment.
