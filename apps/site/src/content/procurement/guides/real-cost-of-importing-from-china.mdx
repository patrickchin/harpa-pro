A chair quoted at $60 can cost considerably more by the time it reaches
your project. The supplier's price may leave out transport, inspection,
import charges, and local delivery. You need the full order budget before
deciding whether the purchase works.

Landed cost combines the product price with the charges needed to get the
goods to the agreed destination. The U.S. International Trade Administration
includes freight, insurance, tariffs, taxes, and other fees in that estimate.
Your own budget should also show order-specific preparation costs, such as
samples, separately so they remain visible.
[International Trade Administration: total export price](https://www.trade.gov/determine-total-export-price)

## Define where your estimate ends

Write the delivery endpoint at the top of the budget: your receiving
warehouse, the project site, or another named address. A port quotation and
a site-delivery quotation cover different work.

Record the supplier's delivery term and named place. Ask the supplier and
forwarder to mark what their prices include. If the supplier already pays
for one transport leg, adding the same charge again overstates the budget.
An unexplained exclusion can produce the opposite problem.

Also define what happens after arrival. Unloading, storage, assembly,
installation, and movement to upper floors may be separate project costs.
Decide whether to include them in the procurement budget or in another
clearly identified allowance.

## Build the budget in the order costs arise

Group charges into preparation, goods, China-side handling, international
transport, destination clearance, and final delivery. Keep the original
quotation or estimate beside each figure.

For each charge, record the payer, currency, amount, evidence date, and
whether the price is confirmed or estimated. A missing quotation should
remain marked as missing. Enter zero only when you have confirmed that
the charge does not apply or is included elsewhere.

Ask the destination broker to confirm the classification, valuation basis,
duties, taxes, and other assessments for your exact goods. There is no
single import percentage you can apply to every order from China. Customs
rules and product classifications affect the result.
[International Trade Administration: import tariffs and fees](https://www.trade.gov/import-tariffs-fees-overview)

Use the country sections of our [main buying guide](/procurement/how-to-buy-from-china#1-check-port-clearance-and-import-requirements)
to identify destination questions. Obtain a current calculation for your
shipment before approving the purchase.

## Work through a hypothetical chair order

The following figures are invented solely to show the method. They are
not current quotations, market averages, tax rates, or a record of a Harpa
Pro order. The example assumes 100 chairs delivered to one receiving
address. Every figure is expressed in U.S. dollars for comparison.

| Cost item | Hypothetical amount |
|---|---:|
| 100 chairs at $60 each | $6,000 |
| Samples and sample courier | $180 |
| Inspection | $250 |
| China inland transport | $220 |
| Export handling and documents | $180 |
| International freight | $900 |
| Cargo insurance | $70 |
| Destination handling and broker | $400 |
| Import duty allowance | $700 |
| Import tax allowance | $1,100 |
| Delivery to receiving address | $200 |
| Payment and currency costs | $90 |
| **Estimated cash outlay before reserve** | **$10,290** |
| Separate contingency reserve | $500 |
| **Cash budget including reserve** | **$10,790** |

The duty and tax lines are hypothetical lump-sum allowances. They have not
been calculated using a real country's tariff or tax formula. Replace them
with the broker's actual estimate, including the stated calculation basis.

The estimated cash outlay is $102.90 per chair before the reserve. The
cash budget including the reserve is $107.90 per chair. These figures
include the example's sample and inspection costs allocated to this order.
They exclude installation and any costs arising after the named delivery
endpoint.

The reserve is money set aside for uncertainty. It becomes an actual cost
only when spent. Keep it separate when reviewing the final result.

## Separate cash required from recoverable amounts

An import tax may need paying at clearance even when your business can
later recover it. Ask your local accountant whether any tax is recoverable,
which documents are required, and when recovery could occur.

Show the cash payment in the cash budget. Record any expected recovery
separately, with its basis and timing. Do not subtract an assumed refund
from the money available to clear the goods.

Apply the same discipline to a refundable sample charge or deposit. Keep
it in the payment plan until the credit or refund has been agreed and can
be reconciled to the supplier's invoice.

## Check the quantities behind the unit cost

Divide the complete order cost by the quantity you can actually use. If
the hypothetical order cost $10,290 but only 97 chairs were usable, its
cost per usable chair would be about $106.08 before any remedy or recovery.
That is a sensitivity example, not a predicted defect rate.

For flooring and tiles, distinguish purchased area from installed coverage.
Cuts, breakage, pattern matching, and spare stock affect the quantities
needed for a project. Agree those allowances with the designer or installer
before comparing a domestic supplier with an imported order.

When several products share freight, choose an allocation method that fits
the charge. Volume may be useful for bulky furniture; weight may matter
for dense stone. Document the method instead of dividing every cost evenly
across unrelated items.

## Use the worksheet with current evidence

[Download the landed-cost worksheet (CSV)](/downloads/procurement/landed-cost-worksheet.csv)
and enter your own estimates in one comparison currency. Keep the original
currencies and exchange-rate evidence in the notes or supporting quotations.
The example column follows the chair order above.

The CSV is an editable cost table with fixed example results. Enter your
own figures and calculate the totals using the notes beside each row.
Keep an unknown amount blank until you obtain an estimate.

Check the budget when the specification changes, when packing dimensions
are confirmed, and before the final shipping booking. Reconcile actual
invoices after delivery. Mark costs already included in another quotation
so they are counted once.

Approve the order when the remaining unknowns have an owner and a funded
allowance. A useful budget shows which figures still need confirmation
and how much cash the next payment stage will require.
