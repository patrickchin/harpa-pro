Putting your name on an existing cabinet handle and developing a new
handle profile are different purchasing projects. One may mainly involve
artwork and packaging. The other can require drawings, tooling, prototypes,
testing, and several rounds of approval.

Describe the changes you need before asking a supplier for “custom” or
“private label” production. Suppliers use these labels differently.
A precise scope makes quotations easier to compare.

## Separate branding, adaptation, and new development

Use a written change list. Identify which parts of the supplier's existing
product remain the same and which parts will change.

| Approach | Example | Main questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private label | Existing fitting with approved branding and packaging | Can you lawfully use the product and brand it? What artwork and order quantities apply? |
| Adaptation | Existing chair in a different fabric or finish | Does the change affect construction, testing, lead time, or replacement parts? |
| Custom development | A new handle profile made from your drawings | Who develops and owns the drawings, prototypes, tooling, and resulting rights? |

Terms such as OEM and ODM do not settle these details. Ask the supplier
to explain what they design, what they manufacture, and what they are
authorised to sell. Record the answer in the purchasing documents.

## Start with the requirement that creates value

For an interior project, a new dimension may be essential because the
product must fit a specific opening. A unique finish might be needed to
match adjoining metalwork. A custom carton may be useful only for retail
presentation.

List the requirements that cannot change. Then consider whether an
existing product can satisfy them. Ask for a separate price and lead-time
effect for each proposed modification. That lets you remove an expensive
change that adds little value.

Avoid selecting a catalogue product solely from a photograph. Request
drawings, material details, dimensions, applicable test evidence, and a
physical sample suitable for your intended use.

## Make the development quotation complete

Request separate amounts for development, samples, tooling, testing,
artwork, packaging, and production. Identify which fees are refundable
or credited against a later order, if any. Put that agreement in writing.

The quotation should also state:

- The design inputs you must supply and their required format.
- The number of prototype or sample revisions included.
- Which changes trigger an additional charge.
- Minimum quantities by model, finish, and packaging version.
- The conditions that start each production or development stage.
- How rejected samples and failed tests will be handled.
- Storage, maintenance, and replacement arrangements for tooling.

Compare the full launch cost and the cost of repeat orders. An affordable
first batch may depend on tooling that cannot be transferred or on a
component the supplier plans to discontinue.

## Resolve ownership before sharing detailed files

Ask who owns existing designs and who will own any work created for
your project. Paying for a mould or drawing does not, by itself, answer
every ownership, access, or usage question. Agree the rights and practical
arrangements in a suitable contract.

WIPO explains that industrial design rights concern a product's appearance
and are territorial. Protection in one country does not automatically
protect the design everywhere. Public disclosure can also affect the
ability to obtain protection, depending on the applicable law.
[WIPO's industrial-design guidance](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/designs/faq-industrial-designs)

Before disclosing valuable files, get advice on the countries and rights
relevant to your project. IP Australia's China guidance recommends due
diligence and suitable agreements when working with manufacturing partners.
The exact contractual approach needs review for your transaction.
[IP Australia's guidance on collaborating in China](https://www.ipaustralia.gov.au/international-ip/how-to-apply-for-ip-overseas/ip-in-china/collaborating-in-china)

For tooling, record an identifier, physical location, permitted users,
maintenance responsibilities, expected condition, and release or transfer
procedure. Ask what happens if you stop ordering or change manufacturers.

## Protect confidential information in practice

Share only the information needed at each stage. Mark confidential files,
control who receives them, and keep a record of issued revisions.
Clarify whether subcontractors may receive drawings or components and
what obligations apply to them.

Confidentiality agreements are one measure among several. WIPO describes
trade-secret protection as depending in part on reasonable steps to keep
valuable information secret. Those steps can include contractual
restrictions and access controls.
[WIPO's trade-secret guidance](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/trade-secrets/tradesecrets_faqs)

Keep design ownership and third-party rights in view. A supplier's ability
to reproduce a well-known product is insufficient evidence that you can
lawfully import or sell it. Resolve permission and clearance questions
before committing to production.

## Approve the product in stages

Agree the approval sequence with the supplier. A useful sequence might
include drawings, a material or finish sample, a prototype, a production
sample, and inspection of the completed batch. Match the stages to the
product's complexity and consequences of failure.

Give every approved drawing and sample a reference. Record dimensions,
tolerances, colour or finish references, materials, components, packaging,
and required performance. Define who can authorise a change.

For a lighting product, replacing a component or modifying construction
can affect the relevance of existing test evidence. Ask a qualified
testing or compliance provider which evidence applies to the exact final
configuration and destination. Complete that check before shipment.

Approval of appearance alone should not release an order whose mechanical
or electrical requirements remain unresolved.

## Plan the second order while negotiating the first

Ask how the supplier will reproduce the approved finish, identify the
correct files, maintain tooling, and source replacement components.
Agree whether a new sample or test review is needed after a material,
component, or manufacturing change.

Keep your approved specification and production records independently
of the supplier's account or messaging history. Before placing a repeat
order, confirm the revision, price, available materials, and production
conditions. A clear record makes it possible to buy the same product
again or explain precisely why the next batch must change.
